IPG Laser Cooling Systems

IPG lasers can generally be divided into two main categories according to their cooling method:

  • Air cooling
  • Water cooling

The primary purpose of a cooling system is to remove heat generated within the optical, electronic, and power components of the laser during operation.

Air Cooling

In air-cooled systems, heat is dissipated by means of heat sinks and cooling fans. Thermal energy is transferred from internal components to the surrounding air through forced convection.

Advantages: simple design; no chiller; no water circuit; low operating costs; minimal maintenance.

Disadvantages: limited heat removal; sensitivity to ambient temperature; fan noise; larger heat sinks at higher power.

For air-cooled lasers, infrastructure requirements are generally minimal. Most specifications only indicate the cooling method (Forced Air or Air Cooling) together with the allowable environmental operating conditions. All cooling elements (heat sinks, fans, air ducts) are integrated into the laser; no external cooling system is required.

Water Cooling

In water-cooled systems, heat generated by the laser’s electronic, optical, and power components is removed by a circulating coolant. The cooling medium is typically tap water or deionized water (DI water) that meets the required water-quality specifications.

Tap water

  • Electrical conductivity: 30–2500 µS/cm
  • Water hardness: < 2.68 mmol/l CaCO3

DI water

  • Electrical conductivity: 30–50 µS/cm
  • Water hardness: < 0.0445 mmol/l CaCO3

Additional requirements

  • pH: 6.5–9.5
  • Turbidity: < 1.0 NTU
  • Oxidizability: < 5 mg/l O2
  • Low concentrations of chlorides, sulfates, metals, and other contaminants
  • Compliance with microbiological limits to prevent biological contamination of the cooling circuit

The main advantage of water compared with air is its significantly higher heat capacity and thermal conductivity, allowing large heat loads to be removed while maintaining stable operating temperatures.

Cooling-System Parameters for Laser Specifications

For preliminary laser specification generation, calculated values of power consumption and cooling-system heat load are used. The software implements the same relations.

Maximum Power Consumption

Maximum Power Consumption is the maximum active electrical power drawn by the laser from the supply. For preliminary estimation:

Pmax = P / η

where Pmax is maximum power consumption (W), P is nominal laser output power (W), and η is electrical efficiency.

Practical data for the U3 series indicate an electrical efficiency of approximately 38% (η = 0.38).

Example, 8 kW laser:

Pmax = 8000 / 0.38 ≈ 21.05 kW

For specification purposes the rounded typical value may be taken as 21–23 kW, and up to 25 kW for the maximum value (about 10–15% above typical, as an operational safety margin).

When preparing specifications:

  • Typical Power Consumption — close to actual operating consumption;
  • Maximum Power Consumption — approximately 10–15% higher than the typical value.

The software reports Pmax = P / η without the extra 10–15% rounding. That margin, if required, is applied when writing the customer specification.

Maximum Apparent Power Consumption

Smax = Pmax / PF

where Smax is apparent power (VA) and PF is the power factor. For U3-series lasers PF is typically 0.90–0.95. The software default is PF = 0.93.

Example, 8 kW, η = 0.38, PF = 0.93:

Smax ≈ 21.05 / 0.93 ≈ 22.6 kVA

Chiller Cooling Capacity (Heat Load)

The chiller must remove the power converted into heat:

Q = Pmax − P = P (1/η − 1)

Example, 8 kW laser: Q ≈ 21.05 − 8.00 = 13.05 kW.

Example, 10 kW laser: Pmax ≈ 26.32 kW, Q ≈ 16.32 kW.

The software uses this Q as the thermal load on the cooling water (energy balance and wall-temperature estimate).

Water Flow

For preliminary selection of water-cooling parameters, the following engineering rule of thumb is used:

recommended flow = 5 L/min per kW of P

Laser powerRecommended water flow
1 kW5 L/min
3 kW15 L/min
4 kW20 L/min
5 kW25 L/min
6 kW30 L/min
8 kW40 L/min
10 kW50 L/min

Such flow rates typically limit the coolant temperature rise across the laser to only a few degrees Celsius during nominal operation.

This rule of thumb is a specification guideline for the laser as a whole. Hydraulic pressure drop of a given heat sink (for example ML-770) is calculated at the flow actually circulating through that plate, which may be lower (examples below: 10–30 L/min). The software shows both: recommended flow from the 5 L/min/kW rule, and ΔP at the entered plate flow.

Water Pressure

Required water pressure is not determined by laser power alone. It depends on the hydraulic resistance of the cooling circuit:

  • inner tube diameter
  • developed tube length
  • number and radius of U-bends
  • fittings and connections
  • coolant flow rate

The software calculates the heat-sink pressure drop ΔP from tube geometry and flow. It does not equal the specification line “Recommended water pressure 2.5–5.0 bar”, which also includes hoses, couplings, filters, valves, the chiller heat exchanger, and a design safety margin (Stage 3).

Psystem, min ≈ ΔP + ΔPhoses + ΔPfilter + ΔPchiller + margin

Cooling Plate (Heat Sink)

The hydraulic characteristics of a laser cooling system depend not only on coolant flow but also on the geometry of the cooling plate and heat-exchanger tube. Different laser models may use different housings, plates, tube layouts, lengths, diameters, and numbers of bends. Required water pressure can therefore differ between platforms at similar output power.

Example: YLR-2000-U3 / ML-770

Software: Laser Water Cooling

The software is a coupled thermal-hydraulic calculator. The thermal model determines heat load from laser power and efficiency and the resulting water temperature rise. The hydraulic model evaluates velocity, Reynolds number, friction, local losses at U-bends and fittings, and pressure drop through the heat-sink tube.

2. Thermal Model

Chiller cooling capacity

Q = Pmax − P

Required chiller cooling capacity is equal to Q.

Coolant temperature rise

ΔT = Q / (ṁ cp)

where ṁ = ρ V̇ is mass flow (kg/s) and cp is specific heat of water (J/kg·K). Water properties are taken at the mean bulk temperature Tin + ΔT/2 (iterated).

Tout = Tin + ΔT

Wall temperature (additional to the original specification list)

Newton’s law of cooling on the inner surface S = π d L:

Q = α S (Tw − Tb)

The heat-transfer coefficient is α = Nu λ / d.

Laminar (Re < 2300). Graetz number Gz = Re Pr d/L. Hausen / Sieder–Tate developing-flow form with fully developed floor Nu = 4.36 (uniform heat flux):

Nulam = max( 4.36 , 1.86 Gz1/3 )

Turbulent (Re ≥ 4000). Gnielinski with Petukhov friction:

fPetukhov = [0.79 ln Re − 1.64]−2

NuGn = (f/8)(Re − 1000) Pr / [ 1 + 12.7 √(f/8) (Pr2/3 − 1) ]

At Re ≥ 4000 the program takes max(Nulam(Re=2300), NuGn). In the transitional band 2300–4000, Nu is linearly interpolated between Nulam(2300) and NuGn(4000).

U-bend (coil) correction. Length-weighted mix of straight-tube Nu and a Gnielinski/VDI helical-coil factor. Coil diameter Dc = 2R; bend fraction fb = min(1, NU π R / L):

Fcoil = 1 + 3.6 (1 − d/Dc) (d/Dc)0.8 (clamped 1.0…1.8)

Nu = Nustraight [ (1 − fb) + fb Fcoil ]

Conduction through the cylindrical tube wall is added:

Rwall = ln(do/d) / (2π λtube L)

Tw = Tb + Q (1/(α S) + Rwall)

Cast-aluminium plate and serpentine routing

The selected catalog plate fixes diameter, wall, developed length, U-bends and routing. Charts vary only the chiller inlet pressure. Developed length comes from the straight runs plus the U-bends plus two edge stubs on the drawing layout.

Heat flows laser → aluminium → stainless wall → water. Aluminium conduction is a buried-pipe plus inter-tube spreading estimate (not a finite-element map of the laser hotspot):

RAl = [ ln(max(t / ro, 1.05)) + ln+(p / (π ro)) ] / (2π λAl L)

with cast-alloy λAl ≈ 150 W/(m·K) (EN AC-AlSi10Mg(Fe), not pure aluminium). The plate temperature compared with the wall limit is

TAl = Tb + Q (Rconv + Rwall + RAl)

Plate mass does not change the steady temperature. It only sets the lumped time constant τ = Rtotal m cp,Al (cp,Al ≈ 900 J/(kg·K)). The drawing leak test (7.5–8.5 bar air) is a tightness test, not the operating pump head.

3. Hydraulic Model

Flow velocity

v = V̇ / A ,  A = π d2 / 4

Reynolds number

Re = ρ v d / μ

  • Re < 2300: laminar
  • 2300 ≤ Re < 4000: transitional
  • Re ≥ 4000: turbulent

Example tables at 20 °C water properties give Re ≈ 19 500 at 10 L/min in the 10.9 mm tube. The software uses viscosity at the mean water temperature, so Re is higher when Q is large (hotter, thinner water).

Friction factor

Laminar: f = 64 / Re. Turbulent (Blasius, smooth tube):

f = 0.3164 / Re0.25

Transitional Re is linearly interpolated between the two.

Friction pressure drop

ΔPfriction = f (L / d) (ρ v2 / 2)

Local pressure losses

ΔPlocal = K (ρ v2 / 2)

K = Kfittings + NU K180

Kfittings = 1.5 (inlet/outlet, NPT 3/8). Each 180° U-bend uses Ito’s 90° coefficient times 1.8:

K90 = 0.131 + 0.163 (d / R)3.5 ,  K180 = 1.8 K90

with R/d clamped to ≥ 0.5. For ML-770, NU = 7 and R = 28 mm.

Total heat-sink pressure drop

ΔP = ΔPfriction + ΔPlocal = (f L/d + K) ρ v2 / 2

This ΔP is the pressure required to push the solved flow through the heat sink only. The chiller set-point is the plate inlet pressure Pin; flow is inverted from ΔP = Pin − Pout.

Inlet pressure at the plate is Pin = Pout + ΔP. Two hydraulic circuits are supported:

  • Dump to reservoir — water leaves the plate into an open tank. Pout = 0, so Pin = ΔP.
  • Return to chiller — water goes back to the chiller, is cooled, and is pumped again. Pout is the return / plate-outlet backpressure if known; leave 0 if unknown (then Pin = ΔP as well). Hoses, filter, and the chiller curve remain Stage 3.

Statement for documentation

The software calculates hydraulic pressure losses in the heat-exchanger tube from geometry, coolant properties, and operating flow rate. That pressure drop is the basis for the heat-sink part of the water-pressure specification. Combined with the thermal model it provides water temperature rise, outlet temperature, and chiller cooling capacity. Full-loop recommended pressure (2.5–5.0 bar) still requires Stage 3 components and a safety margin.

This approach generates cooling-system figures from engineering calculations and actual tube geometry rather than from empirical estimates alone.
